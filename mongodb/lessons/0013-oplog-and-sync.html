The oplog and how secondaries sync

Lesson 6 ended with "the secondaries pull the entry". Today: what an oplog entry really contains, how a secondary fetches, writes and applies it, and what "replication lag" measures.

Your win today: a secondary is behind. You can name which of the three steps (fetch, write, apply) is slow, say how long the oplog will last before the secondary falls off it, and predict which write concerns still work.

1 · What the oplog holds

The oplog is a capped collection, local.oplog.rs. Every write on the primary adds entries to it in the same storage transaction as the write itself (lesson 6). Secondaries replay these entries. The replication README says the entries "are rewritten from the initial operation to make them idempotent".repl README Here is what I saw on store.reviews after these writes:

You ranOplog entry (trimmed)Notice
updateOne({_id}, {$inc: {rating: 1}})op:"u", o:{$v:2, diff:{u:{rating:4}}}, o2:{_id:…}The result (4), not "+1". Replaying it twice gives the same state.
updateOne({_id}, {$push: {tags: "battery"}})o:{$v:2, diff:{stags:{a:true, u1:"battery"}}}"In array tags, set index 1 to battery". Not "append".
insertOne({review:"oplog demo", …})op:"i", o:{_id:…, review:…, rating:5, …}The full document.
deleteOneop:"d", o:{_id:…}Only the key.
updateMany({_id: {$gte: …}}, {$set: {verified: false}})One op:"u" entry per documentA multi-document write is many entries, each with its own ts.

Each entry also has ts (a timestamp: seconds + a counter), t (the election term), ns, the collection ui and wall (wall-clock time). The diff format ($v: 2) is why a one-field change to a big review costs bytes, not the whole document.

Two more facts from the lab:

  • No-op updates write nothing. updateMany({}, {$set: {verified: false}}) on 100,000 reviews matched 100,000 but modified 70,090 (the rest were already false), and the oplog grew by exactly 70,090 entries: 12.2 MB, about 182 B per entry.
  • The oplog is sized on disk, not by time. In my container it was 1,954 MB (the default is 5% of free disk, within bounds the manual sets at 990 MB to 50 GB).oplog size How long that lasts depends on your write rate (section 4).

2 · Three jobs on the secondary, step by step

Secondaries pull. The primary never pushes. In 8.0 the README splits a secondary's work into separate threads with an in-memory queue between them:life as a secondary

  1. Fetch. The fetcher keeps one connection to the sync source and runs a tailable getMore on local.oplog.rs that waits for new data. On the primary, $currentOp shows these as op: "getmore", appName: "OplogFetcher", one per secondary: I saw two. The reply carries metadata (term, the commit point) as well as the entries.oplog fetching
  2. Write. Since 8.0, a dedicated OplogWriter writes the batch to the secondary's own oplog, tells the storage engine to flush the journal, and advances lastWritten.oplog persistence (replSetGetStatus shows writtenOpTime, appliedOpTime and durableOpTime separately.)
  3. Apply. The applier batches entries, splits them among writer threads, and applies them to collections and indexes. "The only restriction ... has to do with the documents": operations on one document stay on one thread, in order; different documents run in parallel. Commands like dropDatabase get a batch of their own. Then it advances lastApplied.oplog application
  4. Report. The secondary tells its sync source its positions. The primary needs these to answer w: 2 or w: "majority".

Where does a secondary fetch from? Not always the primary. With chainingAllowed: true (the default) it picks the closest eligible member by ping time. In my lab both secondaries chose the primary (syncSourceHost in rs.status()), since all pings were 0 ms. It skips members that are too far behind (maxSyncSourceLagSecs) and members whose oldest oplog entry is newer than its own last entry.sync source selection

3 · What the lab showed

3-node replica set in Docker (mongo:8.0.34), 100,000 reviews, all members on one machine.

TestResult
updateMany touching 70,090 reviews780 ms on the primary; both secondaries caught up about 450 ms after the primary finished
fsyncLock() on one secondary, then writes on the primaryw: 1 median 1 ms; w: "majority" median 1 ms (the other secondary still acknowledges); lag of the frozen member grew to 5 s
w: 3, wtimeout: 2000 with that member frozenMongoWriteConcernError: waiting for replication timed out (the write itself stayed on the primary and the unfrozen secondary)
fsyncUnlock()Lag 0 s on the next sample, less than a second later

Four things to take from it. First, with 3 members majority = 2 of 3, so one slow secondary doesn't slow majority writes; w: 3 does. Second, lag is measured in operation time (next section). Third, the secondaries recovered in well under a second once they could apply: they apply in parallel, so a pause costs less time than the primary needed to produce the writes. Fourth, a write timing out on write concern is not a failed write: it succeeded on the primary (lesson 6).

4 · What "lag" measures, and the oplog window

Replication lag is the difference between the primary's newest optime and the secondary's (optimeDate in replSetGetStatus, or rs.printSecondaryReplicationInfo()). It's in operation time, not wall-clock delay. Two consequences:

  • Right after a long idle gap, the first write makes lag look huge for an instant: I saw "8 s" for a burst whose secondaries were fully caught up 450 ms later, because the secondaries' last entry was 8 s older. An idle primary writes a no-op entry about every 10 s so lag stays meaningful.
  • It doesn't say which step is slow. Compare writtenOpTime, appliedOpTime and durableOpTime on the secondary: written far ahead of applied means the applier is the bottleneck (disk, cache pressure from lessons 3 and 5); written behind the primary's newest means fetching (network, sync source busy).

The oplog window is how much history the oplog holds: its size ÷ your write rate in bytes. Using my numbers, that bulk update wrote 12.2 MB; the 1,954 MB oplog would hold about 160 of them. If a secondary stays down longer than the window, the oldest entry on its sync source is newer than the secondary's newest entry: it can't catch up from the oplog and needs a full resync (initial sync). The README describes this as the sync source being "too far ahead" and the secondary waiting for manual intervention.sync source probing Check yours with rs.printReplicationInfo() ("log length start to end").

7 · So in production you should…

Rule: treat the oplog window as a capacity number, like disk space. Know its length in hours at your peak write rate, and keep it longer than your longest planned secondary downtime plus the time to fix a problem.

  1. Measure the window: rs.printReplicationInfo() at peak (a bulk backfill like the updateMany above eats it fast). Resize with replSetResizeOplog if it's short.
  2. Use w: "majority" for data you can't lose, not w: N with N = all members. The lab: one frozen member made w: 3 time out and majority unaffected.
  3. Alert on lag in two parts: writtenOpTime − appliedOpTime (applier/disk) and primary newest − writtenOpTime (fetch/network). Don't alert on a single lag number from an idle set.
  4. Batch big writes (a few thousand per batch, then check lag) instead of one huge updateMany: each modified document is one oplog entry and one apply step, and the window shrinks while you run it.
  5. Make writes change nothing when nothing changes. The no-op updates in the lab cost no oplog. Filter on {verified: true} before setting verified: false if you want the match count to equal the work.
  6. Reads from secondaries lag by definition. A review a user just posted may not be there yet; use read your own writes via the primary, or causal consistency (lesson 14).
